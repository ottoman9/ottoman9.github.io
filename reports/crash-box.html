Crash Box Oblique-Impact Robustness: Anchor Reproduction, a Mode-Flip Diagnosis, and a Priced Design Fix in Abaqus/Explicit

Author: Usman Bashir, BSc Metallurgical and Materials Engineering Date: 2026-08-23 Solver: Abaqus 2024, Explicit (plus Standard for buckling eigenmodes), 4 cores

Internal working records cited in this report (for example DECISIONS, RESULTS_LOG and the sources ledger) are available on request.

Reading order. Section 0 is a self-contained executive summary; read it alone if you read nothing else. Sections 1 to 10 were written when the study closed with a mass-neutral fix worth about two degrees and no energy. Sections 11 to 14 were added after further rounds (a paid-mass round, a plain-tube reference with a two-by-two factorial, two flange studies, and a designed experiment) that produced a working design and overturned the project's central assumption about WHERE the failure lives. Section 12 carries the restated conclusion; Section 13 records a headline claim this project withdrew after auditing itself. The earlier sections are kept unchanged as the record of how the result was reached, per working rule 4.

0. Executive summary

Self-contained. Every statement here is carried from a numbered section below, and nothing is claimed here that is not established there.

The problem, and why it is still open

A crash box is the short thin-walled steel tube between a car's bumper beam and its frame rail. In a crash it is meant to crumple fold by fold, like a concertina, absorbing energy so the expensive structure behind it survives. They are designed and certified overwhelmingly for head-on impact, and head-on they work well.

Real crashes are rarely head-on. Past some critical load angle the neat folding becomes unstable and the box snaps sideways into global bending, hinging like a bent straw instead of crumpling. When that happens the energy it was supposed to eat goes into the rail and the cabin instead. The published literature reports large gains for clever crash box designs, but almost always at zero degrees, and almost always without enforcing the constraint that decides whether a design is usable: the peak crush force, the initial force spike the box transmits, which is what injures occupants and what a manufacturer will not allow to rise.

This study asks three questions in order, each earned before the next: can the model reproduce a published experiment, where does a production-class box lose its folding, and can that boundary be pushed outward without paying in mass or peak force.

What the model is licensed to say

The model was validated against a published oblique crush experiment before anything else was allowed to run. It reproduces energy absorption to within about 10 percent, fold counts to within one fold, and the deformation mode everywhere in the validation range, but it over-predicts peak crush force under edge contact at 10 degrees and above by roughly 20 percent, and only 3 of 7 published cases met the tolerance band declared in advance. That band was never widened after the fact.

So this report may claim energy against angle, the location and sharpness of the mode change, and baseline-versus-modified comparisons in matched configuration where a systematic bias cancels. It may not claim an absolute peak crush force at a given angle as a validated number, and wherever peak force appears it is reported strictly as a ratio against the baseline (DECISIONS.md D3).

The four results

1. The box stops folding at 6.25 degrees, and the transition is sharp. Not a gentle degradation: the fold ratio falls roughly ten times faster across the transition than below it, and two different starting imperfections agree to 0.24 percent. This number is a lower bound, not a measurement. Section 11.3 established why: the failure is governed by the end attachment, not the tube, and the modelled attachment is a free-standing 1.8 mm flange where a real box bolts to a stiffer rail. A better-represented joint would move the number outward.

2. It is the attachment, not the tube, that governs the oblique failure. A plain tube unexpectedly beat the production-representative baseline; a two-by-two factorial attributed the whole effect to the end flange rather than the section shape. This reframes the project and is its most transferable engineering result. It also comes with a firm negative: the attachment cannot be exploited. Four independent routes to stiffen it (removing the flange, thickening it, stiff flange plus a base trigger, and gussets at two sizes) all buy angle performance and all break the peak force cap, because anything resisting the hinge at the joint also resists the fold that starts there.

3. A design that works, priced. PW adds a short internal web across the loaded third of the box plus two embossed bead bands, for plus 5 percent mass and no worse peak force (118.42 kN against the baseline's 118.19, inside the declared 1 percent cap). It removes the energy cliff: energy retention at 10 degrees rises from the baseline's 70 percent to 94 percent, energy at 10 degrees is plus 34 percent, and best specific energy absorption gain is plus 28 percent. Unlike the free mass-neutral fix it still gains at severe speed (56 km/h), where that fix would have been rejected. At the angle the industry actually tests at, RCAR's 15 km/h 10 degree condition, PW absorbs the impact in about a quarter less stroke than the baseline, 56.8 mm against 76.0 mm, and that saving holds across every plausible assumption about how the crash energy splits.

4. Hand-picking had already found the plateau. A 25-point designed experiment over PW's web thickness, bead depth, bead position and band spacing, 50 solves, ranks PW second out of twenty-five, and the point that beats it does so by 0.83 percent. That is inside the 3 percent threshold declared before the study ran, so the recorded result is that PW's hand-picked parameters were already near optimal.

Three findings that transfer beyond this part

Deformation mode and energy absorption are largely independent levers. The project's founding assumption was that stopping the sideways collapse would recover the lost energy. Six design candidates showed it does not, and the designed experiment later found the same thing independently: designs that bend can out-absorb designs that fold.

Weaken precisely what was going to fail, and nothing else. Every candidate that removed material from a region already carrying load lost energy. The winners added or triggered locally, at the loaded end, leaving the rest alone.

Strength-based fixes reproduce the literature's gains and break the constraint the literature does not apply. Real multi-cell construction gave the largest energy gain in the project, plus 83 percent at 10 degrees, together with the largest violation, plus 87 percent peak force. That pattern repeated across three unrelated candidates. The published gains are real; they would be rejected by a peak force constraint.

The honest limits

  • The fix has a band. PW is a fix for roughly 6 to 20 degrees. Beyond 20 it converges on the baseline, and by 30 degrees the two are indistinguishable.
  • Golf-class, not Golf-exact. The baseline geometry is a documented nominal standing in for a production C-segment box, not a measured part, so every claim is about the family.
  • No validated absolute peak force, per the licence above.
  • The response surface deliverable does not work. The fitted surface scored R2 = 0.715 on its own data and Q2 = -8.35 under cross-validation, meaning it predicts unseen designs worse than a constant would, because the design space contains a mode boundary that no polynomial can represent. It is reported as a failure rather than dressed up, and the verification run confirmed the prediction that it was wrong by two thirds.
  • A headline claim was withdrawn. An earlier version reported that PW passes the RCAR condition the baseline fails. An audit found that verdict had been evaluated over a 60 mm post-processing window while the text claimed the full 100 mm stroke; against the stated criterion the baseline also passes. The claim was withdrawn and replaced with the intrusion result above, which needs neither the energy-split assumption nor a stroke-limit assumption and is therefore stronger. Section 13 records it in full.
  • Also stated in Section 9: a continuous-shell model of what is really a spot-welded box (biased toward cleaner folding), plasticity without damage (biased toward over-prediction of energy), and a secondary regulatory claim that could not be traced to primary text and is therefore used nowhere.

1. Summary

A crash box is the short thin-walled tube between a car's bumper beam and its frame rail. It is meant to crumple fold by fold, eating crash energy so the structure behind it sees a controlled force. Crash boxes are designed and certified overwhelmingly for head-on load, and in that case they work well. Real crashes are rarely head-on. Past some critical load angle the neat folding becomes unstable and the box snaps sideways into global bending, and the energy it was supposed to absorb goes into the rail and the cabin instead.

I set out to answer three questions in order, each having to be earned before the next was allowed. Can an Abaqus/Explicit model reproduce a published oblique crush experiment, against a tolerance band written down before any solve ran? If so, where exactly does a Golf-class production box lose its folding behaviour, and how sharply? And can a design change push that boundary outward without adding mass or raising the head-on force spike?

The three headline results:

  1. The anchor reproduction passed on its axial case and failed its oblique gate. The best axial configuration lands at PCF 42.894 kN against a published 44.02 (-2.6 percent) and EA 0.9203 kJ against 0.913 (+0.8 percent), with three folds matching the published photographs and a clean energy audit. Across all seven published cases, however, only 3 of 7 met the pre-declared plus or minus 10 percent band, and the failures are all in peak force under edge contact. The band was never widened. Section 4 describes the two investigation rounds, the two genuine model faults they exposed, and the structural tension that stopped me going further.
  2. The Golf-class box stops folding at 6.25 degrees, and the transition is sharp and not a numerical accident. The fold ratio falls at -0.191 per degree across the transition against -0.020 per degree below it, roughly an order of magnitude steeper than the surrounding trend. Two different imperfection seeds agree to 0.24 percent on energy and 0.002 on fold ratio, with zero mode disagreements. Energy retention runs 100/94/92/90/84/70/62/49/34/28 percent from 0 to 30 degrees.
  3. A mass-neutral fix buys about two degrees, and only at low speed. Two embossed bead bands near the loaded end move the transition from 6.25 degrees to between 7.5 and 8.75, at identical mass, axial peak force within 0.1 percent and energy within 0.5 percent averaged across the whole sweep. It does not flatten the energy curve, which the brief also asked for. And at 56 km/h its advantage shrinks by 40 percent while costing up to 18.6 percent of energy, so it would be rejected at severe speed.

The finding I did not expect, and which reframes the whole problem, is in Section 6.4: deformation mode and energy absorption turned out to be largely independent levers. Phase B located the mode flip precisely and I assumed, as the brief did, that stopping the flip would recover the energy. Six design candidates showed it does not. At 8.75 degrees the untouched baseline is classified as bending and absorbs 4.786 kJ, while a candidate that folds more cleanly and shortens by the same amount absorbs 3.964 kJ.

The discipline holding this together is pre-registration. Bands, audit thresholds, geometry and success criteria were written into BRIEF.md, DECISIONS.md and RESULTS_LOG.md before the runs that would test them existed. Several predictions I wrote down were refuted, including one about my own correction and one about my own success criterion. They are listed as refuted in Section 8, because a scorecard with no failures in it is evidence that the scorecard was written afterwards.

2. The benchmark, and why I chose it

The oblique anchor is Karantza and Manolakos, Metals 2022, 12(11), 1862 (P4_SOURCES.md A8), open access, CC BY, full 21-page PDF archived locally. Square AA6060-T6 extrusions, 50 mm outer width, 1.5 mm wall, 100 mm long, 78.57 g, crushed 60 mm at 0, 5, 10 and 15 degrees, each in two initial contact conditions (a whole top edge touching first, or a single corner). It publishes the complete material card including an eight-point hardening table, a full PCF/MCF/EA/SEA/CFE table for all seven cases, force-displacement curves at every angle, photographed collapse modes, and its own LS-DYNA deviations.

That last item matters: the paper's own finite element model landed within 0.13 to 7.6 percent on peak force and 1.6 to 6.8 percent on energy. A different code running the same published material card earns a slightly wider band, not a loose one, which is why BRIEF.md Section 6 set plus or minus 10 percent.

The first-choice anchor was Reyes, Langseth and Hopperstad (2002, 2003), the paper this literature traces back to, but its numeric data sits behind a paywall and three retrieval attempts failed. The sources pass recorded that failure explicitly rather than quietly substituting, and the Metals 2022 paper was recovered instead via the publisher's asset CDN after the main site returned 403 to automated fetches. Reyes remains the mechanism backbone in the literature review, not a data source.

A caveat that shaped Phase B: at the anchor's stocky proportions (width over thickness about 33, length over width 2) the experiment shows no mode flip at all up to 15 degrees. Every specimen folded progressively. So the anchor cannot validate flip prediction; it validates forces, fold counts and the absence of a flip where the experiment says there is none. Hunting the flip required the slender, crash-box-proportioned geometry of Phase B, and that transfer of modelling approach rather than numbers is stated as a limitation.

3. Methods

3.1 Geometry

Anchor. Mid-surface square side 48.5 mm, which is the paper's own shell dimensioning and reproduces the published 78.57 g specimen mass to four figures at rho 2700 and t 1.5 mm. The model's total mass came out at 78.570 g, which was the first independent confirmation that the geometry interpretation was right. Length 100 mm. Round 2 added a 2.0 mm mid-surface corner radius (Section 4.4).

Golf-class box. Rectangular thin-walled tube, 80 by 60 mm outer, 180 mm long, 1.8 mm wall, frozen in DECISIONS.md D1 with every value labelled by basis. The outer section and length are nominal, set from typical published C-segment crash box proportions, not Golf VII facts. The wall thickness is an inference band of 1.5 to 2.0 mm from VW's own SSP 520 body programme, which documents the front rails as 2.0 mm inner member plus 1.5 mm closing plate; the box must fold before those rails, so a mid-band 1.8 mm was chosen. A 2.3 mm mid-surface corner radius was carried over from the anchor work, and 12 mm representative end flanges stand in for the bumper-beam and rail-flange attachments (P4_SOURCES.md C5).

I was granted one bounded geometry adjustment before the first solve and declined it (DECISIONS.md D2). No new evidence had appeared, and the one substantive reason to use it, that a longer or more slender box would make the mode flip easier to find, is not a legitimate basis. Choosing geometry to produce the phenomenon you are hunting makes the result circular.

3.2 Materials

Anchor: AA6060-T6 exactly as published, rho 2700, E 70 GPa, nu 0.33, Mises plasticity with the paper's eight-point hardening table from 180 MPa at zero plastic strain to 229 MPa at 5.77 percent, modelled rate independent as the paper models it.

Golf box: DP600 Johnson-Cook. The card in use is not the one the sources pass originally recorded, and the replacement is one of this project's more useful findings. Section 5.2 tells that story.

3.3 Mesh, elements and the imperfection policy

S4R shells with five through-thickness integration points and enhanced hourglass control, matching the paper's five-point Belytschko-Lin-Tsay formulation. Anchor mesh 1.5 mm, settled by a three-rung ladder at 3.0, 2.0 and 1.5 mm plus a second ladder in fully integrated S4 elements. Golf box mesh 2.0 mm, chosen to hold elements-per-fold constant with the converged anchor rung using the Abramowicz-Jones fold half-wavelength formula (P4_SOURCES.md C2).

Imperfection. Every crush model is seeded with the first elastic buckling eigenmode of the same mesh at the same tilt angle, from an Abaqus/Standard *BUCKLE run, scaled to one tenth of wall thickness. The shape comes from the eigenvalue analysis and the amplitude is the conventional 0.1t; neither was chosen by looking at the target. Each tilt angle needs its own eigenvalue job, because *IMPERFECTION stores global-frame nodal displacement vectors and a mode computed on an upright tube would be applied in the wrong direction to a tilted one.

3.4 Loading

Anchor: rigid flat plate at a constant 1 m/s, which is the paper's own approach; it ran its quasi-static tests in explicit at 1 m/s exploiting AA6060-T6's rate insensitivity. The tube is rotated, not the plate, which is what the experiment did.

Golf box: constant 4166.7 mm/s, the RCAR 15 km/h low-speed structural test closing speed, through a fixed 100 mm stroke. This replaced an earlier free-flying impactor design; Section 5.1 explains why that had to change.

Severe condition: 15555.6 mm/s, the UNECE R94 and US NCAP 56 km/h closing speed, 3.73 times the RCAR speed, with everything else held identical.

3.5 Post-processing, fixed before Phase A and applied identically to both sides of every comparison

Force is the vertical reaction at the plate reference node, or for the free-flying runs the impactor deceleration times its mass. Displacement is plate travel from first contact, with a 0.5 mm standoff built into every deck so first contact is unambiguous. EA is the trapezoidal integral of force over the crush window; MCF is EA over the window; CFE is MCF over PCF. That window is 60 mm, inherited from the anchor specimen, and every EA and SEA figure in this report is a 60 mm window quantity. It is a comparative metric, not a stroke limit, and Section 13.1 records what went wrong when the two were confused. PCF is quoted from an SAE J211 CFC 600 filtered channel with the unfiltered peak also recorded, because a prescribed-velocity rigid impactor produces a numerically noisy raw contact force. That filtering decision was fixed before Phase A and it earned its keep at severe speed, where raw peaks reach 183 kN against a filtered 114 kN.

SEA is computed against crushed mass, not total mass. The paper's own EA/SEA pair implies 47.16 g, which is its 78.57 g tube scaled by crush over length (47.14 g), so that is the published convention and both sides of every comparison use it.

3.6 The energy audit, and what it rejected

Every solve reports artificial energy over internal energy (5 percent target, 10 percent hard fail), kinetic over internal, total energy drift (below 1 percent of peak internal, a project declaration stricter than any traced floor), and contact energy sanity (P4_SOURCES.md C6). Thresholds were declared numerically before the first solve.

The audit did real work. It rejected the coarse and medium anchor rungs at 19.5 and 13.5 percent artificial energy, so neither was ever reported as a result. Across 129 solves, no run that failed the audit appears anywhere as a result.

4. Phase A: earning the right to trust the model

4.1 The first fault: element deletion destroyed the tube

The paper carries a 7.93 percent failure plastic strain to capture slight corner tearing. I included the direct Abaqus equivalent, *SHEAR FAILURE with element deletion. The first run deleted 897 of 2112 shell elements, 42 percent of the tube. Energy came out at 0.314 kJ against a published 0.913, and the force history collapsed into repeated zero-force gaps.

The cause is that in a shell with five Simpson points the outermost points sit exactly on the surfaces, where bending strain at a fold hinge of radius about the wall thickness runs to tens of percent, far past 7.93 percent. Deletion therefore cascades through every fold hinge instead of staying at the corners. Plasticity only, with the overprediction bias stated, for everything after (RESULTS_LOG.md D-A1).

4.2 The second fault: a mesh-insensitive error is not a mesh error

The ladder behaved properly on energy: artificial energy fell 19.5, 13.5, 10.2 percent and mean force fell 26.58, 23.57, 22.80 kN with refinement. But peak force did not move at all: 58.95, 58.50, 58.30 kN against a published 44.02.

Two hand checks located it before any more solver time was spent. The section area is 291 mm2, so the published 44.02 kN is a mean axial stress of 151 MPa, below the material's 180 MPa yield, while my 58.5 kN is 201 MPa, above it. The real tubes collapsed before their walls fully yielded and mine did not. An Abaqus/Standard eigenvalue run on the same mesh put first elastic local buckling at 72.4 kN, or 249 MPa, against the classical simply-supported plate value of 247 MPa for that wall, agreeing to within 1 percent. So the model was elastically correct, and a geometrically perfect tube of this material simply has no reason to collapse until it squashes. Real extrusions do, because they are not perfect.

Adding the declared eigenmode imperfection moved peak force from 58.50 to 44.28 kN on the same mesh. Mean force fell from 23.57 to 17.52. Fold count went from about two lobes to three, matching the photographs.

The same round tested contact friction as a controlled comparison: cutting aluminium self-contact from the paper's quoted 1.2 to 0.2, a factor of six, changed mean force by 0.1 percent. That suspicion was closed rather than left hanging.

4.3 The oblique gate failed, and the failure had a pattern

Round 1 passed 2 of 7 cases. The failures were not seven independent misses. Energy was high by a consistent +11.5 percent mean with all seven errors positive, which is a model property rather than scatter. And peak force split cleanly by contact condition: the three corner cases gave -2.4, +11.2 and +9.5 percent while the three edge cases gave +17.0, +28.3 and +27.7.

4.4 Round 2: one correction worked, one backfired

Two corrections, both fixed from engineering basis before any round 2 run and never adjusted afterwards (RESULTS_LOG.md D-A3):

A 2.0 mm mid-surface corner radius, since the paper states outer width and wall but never the corner radius, and extrusion practice puts the outer radius near 1.5 to 2 times wall thickness. Corners are the stiffest part of a square section and carry most of the fold work.

A 0.15 mm out-of-square loaded end, modelled as a linear tilt of the end plane, at the ISO 2768-m tolerance band for a 50 mm sawn feature, aimed at the edge-contact peak force.

I flagged in advance that the second value had no published source and that fixing an unsourced number against known targets is how tuning gets in through the back door. So both were written into the generator before running, and a discriminating prediction was recorded: the end tilt had to move edge contact substantially and leave corner contact nearly alone.

The corner radius worked cleanly. On its own it moved energy from +9.7 to +0.8 percent while costing 2.6 percent of peak force, and dropped artificial energy from 7.68 to 5.33 percent. Both gate metrics inside the band with margin on each side.

The end tilt backfired on the axial case, and by far more than I predicted. An attribution run isolating the two corrections showed the corner radius cost 1.12 kN of peak force and the end tilt cost 4.38 kN, 10 of the 12.5 point drop. I had predicted about 1.5 points, reasoning from 0.18 degrees of effective tilt against the experiment's near-axial sensitivity. It was seven times that.

The discriminating prediction did hold where it was aimed: at 5 degrees the end tilt moved edge peak force by 2.14 kN and corner peak force by 0.30 kN, a 7 to 1 split. So the mechanism is real. Round 2 reached 3 of 7, with energy now inside the band at six of seven cases and fold counts within one fold everywhere.

4.5 Why I stopped

The result is a structural tension, not an unconverged search:

end tilt axial PCF 5 degree edge PCF
0.00 mm -2.6 percent, pass about +17 percent, fail
0.15 mm -12.5 percent, fail +8.5 percent, pass

The axial case wants a perfectly square loaded end and the oblique edge cases want an imperfect one, and one specimen cannot be both. No single value of that parameter satisfies both ends of the anchor set, and re-picking it to trade one against the other is exactly the tuning I had committed against. I reviewed this and took the project forward with the model's licence stated explicitly (DECISIONS.md D3).

The licence, carried into every later claim. The model reproduces energy absorption to roughly 10 percent, fold count to within one fold everywhere, progressive folding with no global bending anywhere in 0 to 15 degrees, the corner-beats-edge energy ordering, and a clean audit on every run. It does not reproduce peak crush force under edge contact at 10 degrees and above. So Phases B and C may claim EA(theta), mode, and matched-configuration comparisons; they may not claim absolute peak force at a given angle as a validated quantity.

4.6 The analytical cross-check

BRIEF.md Section 6 declares a plus or minus 20 percent check against the Abramowicz-Jones square-tube theory on the anchor's axial case. Using sigma0 as the mean of yield and ultimate, M0 = sigma0 h^2/4 and the traced geometric factor, the prediction is 14.28 kN over the full stroke against a measured 15.34 kN: +7.4 percent, pass, consistent with the pre-solve consistency calculation that put experiment and theory within 6 percent.

I also applied the same formula to the Golf box, where it over-predicts by 47 percent. That is an out-of-domain extension rather than a failed gate: the formula assumes a plain square tube, and the Golf box is rectangular, flanged and radiused. An earlier entry in my own results log recorded this as a failed declared check; that entry applied a Phase A gate to Phase B and is corrected in place.

5. Phase B: the diagnosis

5.1 The loading design that would have wasted the phase

The first Phase B configuration was a free-flying 575 kg impactor at the RCAR speed, carrying 4.99 kJ derived from an energy-share chain. The single axial calibration run showed the box stopped it and rebounded after 48.59 mm.

That is a fatal design flaw for a sweep, and it is mine. With a fixed impact energy, EA is pinned at that energy for every angle where the box still stops the impactor. EA(theta), the brief's headline curve, would have come out flat by construction across exactly the range where the critical angle lives. Raising the impactor mass until the axial case used half the stroke would need about 1040 kg, implying a 66 percent energy share for one box, which is not credible.

The fix (RESULTS_LOG.md D-B2) was a constant-velocity impactor at the same RCAR speed through a fixed 100 mm stroke, which mirrors the validated Phase A method exactly, makes EA(theta) directly comparable, and runs 2.3 times faster. Nothing was lost: each force-displacement curve still yields the crush distance at which absorbed energy reaches the RCAR share, which is the intrusion the rail actually sees. Because the axial case was run alone before the batch, this cost one run rather than six.

5.2 The material card was wrong, and the model found it

The same calibration run produced a mean crush force of 102.6 kN against a pre-solve estimate of 54. Evaluating the recorded Johnson-Cook set as a stress-strain curve rather than reading its parameters:

Quantity Recorded set DP600 grade
flow stress at 2 percent plastic strain 781 MPa below UTS, so under about 650
implied engineering UTS 846 MPa 600 to 700 MPa

Its flow stress at 2 percent plastic strain already exceeded DP600's entire UTS. That set describes a DP800 or DP980 class steel. The ledger row had been marked TRACED-MEDIUM with a standing action to verify against the primary before the report stage, and this was that verification failing.

The root cause, established with the coordinator session, is that the recorded set was a garbled hybrid: its A and n came from the true primary's tube fit while its B and m matched nothing in it. The replacement is Thompson 2006 (University of Waterloo MASc thesis), DP600 characterized 0.003 to 1500 /s, whose Table 5.1 sheet fit is A 165, B 968.57, n 0.206, c 0.0145, m 0.868. A crash can is stamped sheet, so the sheet fit is the right column. I verified every number against the archived primary rather than accepting the relay.

One open item came with that relay: the reference strain rate was said to be unstated, leaving an 8 percent ambiguity to be resolved by fitting against a published curve. It is stated outright in the thesis's own modelling chapter, "the reference strain rate in the constitutive models was 0.001 ms-1 because of the unit system", which in an mm-ms-g system is 1.0 per second. So the value is traced, not assumed, and the ambiguity does not exist.

Read at the datasheet rate, the card gives 398 MPa proof and 647 MPa UTS, both inside the DP600 grade definition. The old card missed on both.

What the swap changed, and what it did not. Absolute energy fell 13 to 18 percent at every angle, consistent with a 17 percent softer UTS. Retention barely moved: 70 versus 74 percent at 10 degrees, 49 versus 49 at 20, 34 versus 34 at 25. The shape of the collapse is a property of the geometry, not the steel. Both sweeps are preserved and together form a material-sensitivity comparison.

5.3 The mode discriminator, and why it was validated first

Fold count cannot separate folding from bending, because both produce wall waviness. Lateral drift cannot either, because an obliquely loaded tube moves sideways for purely geometric reasons. I built and discarded two classifiers before arriving at one I trusted. The second failed in an instructive way: it looked for a kink between the tube's half-lengths, which a hinge forming at the fixed end does not produce, so it confidently reported folding where the box had hinged at its base.

The measure finally used is deliberately blunt. In the tube's own frame, take the centroid of the fixed-end ring and the loaded-end ring, and compute how much closer they got along the original axis (shortening) against how far the loaded end swung sideways off it (offset):

fold_ratio = shorten / (shorten + offset)

Folding converts impactor travel into shortening. Bending rotates the box about a hinge, so the ends stay apart while the top swings out.

It was validated on the Phase A anchor before being used, where the published experiment reports progressive folding at every tested angle. It returned folding for all cases, with ratios spanning 0.763 to 0.997. That calibrates the threshold against a known answer instead of asserting one.

At 5 degrees the box shortens 85 mm and drifts 10. At 10 degrees the top swings 92 mm, nearly the whole crush stroke going into rotation. The fold ratio falls at -0.191 per degree across the transition against -0.020 per degree below it and -0.004 to -0.007 per degree above 10 degrees.

The mode change and the energy loss happen at different angles, and this is the single most useful output of Phase B. At 6.25 degrees the box has already stopped folding yet still absorbs 92 percent of its axial energy. The energy penalty arrives between 8.75 and 10 degrees. Crush force efficiency confirms the lag, holding flat at 0.79 to 0.81 through the flip and only dropping at 10 degrees. So a design fix must target hinge formation at about 6 degrees, not the visible energy cliff at 10; aiming at the symptom would aim three degrees too late, at a box that has already failed.

The contrast with Phase A is the physically interesting part. Under oblique load the eccentricity of the load itself breaks the symmetry and swamps a 0.18 mm perturbation. The same modelling choice dominates one phase and is irrelevant in the other, for a reason that makes sense.

5.6 The mechanism: two plastic zones and a rigid middle

Plastic work along the box, binned into tenths of length, 0 at the fixed end:

bins 0 deg 5 deg 6.25 deg 10 deg
0 to 1, base 60.6% 60.2% 60.5% 39.0%
4 to 6, middle 12.1% 5.8% 3.3% 3.6%
7 to 9, loaded end 4.2% 13.2% 23.4% 43.4%

This is not a single mid-length hinge, which is what I had assumed. It is two plastic zones with a middle that stays essentially elastic and rotates as a near-rigid body. Three facts follow. The base carries 60 percent of the plastic work at 0, 5 and 6.25 degrees alike, so it is not what fails. The loaded end goes 4.2 to 43.4 percent as the angle opens, so it is. And the middle third does under 4 percent at every angle past 5 degrees, which is dead material and therefore a mass budget.

It also explains the lag in Section 5.4: at 6.25 degrees the loaded-end zone has formed but the base is still folding normally, so energy holds; by 10 degrees the loaded end dominates and the base is starved.

6.1 Constraints and candidates

Both constraints from BRIEF.md Section 3 are hard: no mass increase over 683.8 g, and no worse axial peak force than 118.2 kN. Six candidates were run, all mass-neutral by construction, each aimed at the loaded-end zone rather than at the energy cliff.

6.2 A success criterion that was declared early and still wrong

The criterion written before solving was: move the transition, hold mass, do not worsen axial peak force. Candidate A satisfies all three literally, with peak force lower at 100.6 kN, and is plainly not a win, because it loses 32 percent of axial energy. The criterion never required preserving the box's primary job.

I added the fourth condition and applied it to all candidates. Declaring criteria in advance is right, and it does not remove the duty to check whether the criterion captures what actually matters.

6.3 Grading disarms triggers, and placement decides affordability

Two comparisons carry the mechanism findings, and both were designed so the answer would be attributable.

D versus E. E is D plus C's beads. They are indistinguishable: energy differs by at most 0.31 percent and fold ratio by at most 0.009 at every angle. C's beads moved the 6.25 degree fold ratio from 0.659 to 0.883 on their own and contribute nothing once the loaded third is thickened to 2.05 mm. A bead works by being locally weak; raising the surrounding thickness removes that contrast. The two fixes cancel rather than combine. D was run separately specifically because Phase A round 2 had already taught the cost of changing two things at once.

F versus G. Identical trigger physics, differing only in placement. F spread four bands down the whole box at the natural fold pitch and lost up to 25.1 percent of energy; G put deeper beads only in the loaded fifth and lost nothing. Triggers reduce crush force, which is their purpose, and force integrated over distance is energy. The design rule: weaken precisely the part that was going to fail, and nothing else.

6.4 The finding I did not predict: mode and energy are separate levers

At 8.75 degrees the untouched baseline is classified as bending and absorbs 4.786 kJ. Candidate F is classified mixed, folds more cleanly, shortens by essentially the same amount (75.6 against 75.0 mm), and absorbs 3.964 kJ. Folding more did not absorb more.

Across all six candidates, every mechanism that moved the mode boundary did so by changing where the box prefers to deform, and the only candidate that genuinely raised energy (B, the internal web) did it by making the box stronger, which spiked axial peak force by 15 percent and broke a hard constraint.

Phase B correctly located the flip. The inference that stopping the flip would recover the energy, which the brief and I both made, does not hold.

G's mean fold-ratio advantage falls from +0.128 to +0.076, a 40 percent reduction. It still folds further than the baseline at every angle, so the effect is not a pure low-speed artifact, but it is materially smaller in the regime that matters most for injury.

The baseline improves on its own at speed, its fold ratio rising by +0.128, +0.067 and +0.043 and its energy by +4.0, +4.8 and +9.4 percent. Inertia stabilizes folding: the box's own mass resists the sideways swing long enough for folds to form. High speed does part of G's job for free.

That is inertia, not material rate hardening, and the traced card proves it. The Johnson-Cook C of 0.0145 predicts +1.9 percent flow stress for this speed ratio. The observed energy change is two to five times larger, so the rate term cannot account for it. G's beads trigger folds early, which is free when inertia is weak, but at high speed the baseline was going to fold anyway, so the beads subtract force without buying mode stability that was still needed.

Verdict: G is a regime-specific win. At the RCAR condition, the regime crash boxes are explicitly sized for, it meets every declared constraint. At 56 km/h it would be rejected.

7. What this is worth, and what it is not

The engineering inference P2 made was a cost saving from a lighter part. P4 cannot make an equivalent claim, and saying so plainly is the honest result.

What the project delivers: a quantified diagnosis of where and how sharply this box class fails under angled load, on a validated discriminator, with the critical angle shown to be seed-independent; a mechanism explanation (two plastic zones, a rigid middle, the loaded end being what changes); a transferable design rule; the mode-energy independence finding; and a mass-neutral change worth about two degrees at low speed.

What it does not deliver: more energy kept out of the frame rail. No candidate achieved that within the constraints, and the one that raised energy broke the peak-force constraint by 15 percent. Since the payoff quantity for repair cost and cabin integrity is energy absorbed, not mode, the practical value of G is limited to the low-speed regime and is best stated as a robustness margin rather than a saving.

Context that needs care: the RCAR low-speed structural test is itself run at a 10 degree barrier angle, beyond this box class's 6.25 degree transition. That is a test condition, not a performance floor, and no traced source sets a required critical angle. The honest observation is that the industry's own low-speed test angle sits past the transition, not that the box fails a published requirement.

8. Predictions I wrote down and got wrong

  1. End-tilt cost. I predicted about 1.5 points of axial peak force from 0.18 degrees of effective tilt. It cost 10 points, seven times more. The model's axial peak force is far more sensitive to out-of-square loading than the experiment's own angle sweep implies.
  2. Artificial energy. I claimed an S4 versus S4R comparison ruled out artificial energy as a source of force overshoot. That comparison confounded element type with mesh size. At equal mesh, about a fifth of the residual is hourglass inflation. Corrected in the log.
  3. The hinge location. I assumed a single mid-length hinge. It is two plastic zones with a rigid middle, and the second zone is at the loaded end.
  4. Mode implies energy. I assumed stopping the flip would recover the energy. Six candidates say otherwise, and the clearest counterexample has a bending baseline out-absorbing a folding candidate.
  5. Combining A and C. I expected the grading and the triggers to be complementary because they failed in opposite regions. The grading disarmed the triggers completely.
  6. The success criterion. Declared before solving, and still missing the requirement that mattered most.
  7. The analytical check. I recorded it as failing when the Golf box came out 47 percent high. That applied a Phase A gate to Phase B; the anchor check passes at +7.4 percent.

Two process errors also belong here. I designed the Phase B loading in a way that would have made the headline curve flat by construction, caught only because a single calibration run preceded the batch. And I changed two model corrections at once in Phase A round 2, which cost an extra diagnostic run to attribute and directly motivated the D-versus-E design in Phase C.

9. Limitations

  1. Geometry is a labelled reconstruction. Claims are about the Golf-class box family, not the exact Golf R part; OEM drawings are not public.
  2. The anchor licence (DECISIONS.md D3): absolute peak force at a given angle is not a validated quantity.
  3. Continuous shell, no spot-welded seam. A real seamed box folds less cleanly, so its true critical angle is likely at or below 6.25 degrees. The estimate is conservative, not flattering.
  4. The DP600 card is a labelled "DP-class crash-can steel" inference; the box's actual grade is not published anywhere found.
  5. Plasticity only, no damage model; no traced damage parameter set exists for this grade. Bias is toward overprediction of energy.
  6. The 100 mm stroke is 56 percent of box length; behaviour beyond that, including densification, is not characterized.
  7. The mode discriminator is calibrated on one anchor geometry. Its threshold should be re-validated before being applied to a very different section.

10. What I would do next

Attack the energy cliff directly, not the mode boundary. The mode-energy independence finding says the two are separate problems, and only the second one is worth money. That means asking what sets the force level once the box has begun to hinge, rather than trying to prevent the hinge.

Test the rigid middle. It does under 4 percent of the plastic work at every angle past 5 degrees. Candidates A and D tried to spend that budget on the loaded end and lost energy doing it. A shorter box, or one whose middle is removed rather than thinned, is a different use of the same observation.

Resolve the edge-contact peak force limitation, which is the one thing blocking a full Phase A pass. The end-flatness hypothesis is live but untested at other amplitudes, and I deliberately did not chase it because the amplitude had no source. A measured specimen would settle it.

Re-run the sweep with a spot-welded seam to test whether the conservative bias in limitation 3 is small or large.

Sections 1 to 10 were written when Phase C closed with candidate G, a mass-neutral fix worth about two degrees and no energy. Three follow-up questions then reopened the work, and the answers changed the conclusion. This section records that, and Section 12 restates the headline.

11.2 The industry comparison, done properly

The published claims (P4_SOURCES.md D1 to D3) run from +27 percent to +213 percent SEA. PW's SEA gain peaks at +27.8 percent at 10 degrees, mean +4.5 percent, and is NEGATIVE below 8 degrees. So PW matches the foam-filled conical figure almost exactly and does not approach the multi-cell or hierarchical numbers.

Four things make that comparison less lopsided than it looks, and three of them are now backed by this project's own runs rather than by argument.

Those gains are measured against a plain square tube. So the plain tube was built in this toolchain, same mass, same material, same conditions (Section 11.3). It is a genuinely different animal from a production-proportioned flanged box.

Most of those studies do not impose a peak force cap. Two candidates here reproduced the literature's energy direction and were rejected on exactly that constraint: candidate B, the full-length internal web (+6.0 percent axial energy, +15.1 percent axial peak force) and candidate TB, the widening taper (+2.9 percent energy, +2.0 percent peak force). That is a claim from runs, not from reading.

The industry's own document is far more sober than the papers. European Aluminium's crash management systems release (2013, archived locally) states that foam-filled sections absorb "slightly more impact energy than empty sections of similar mass" and that "properly designed multi-cell cross sections are more efficient energy absorbers", with foam's real advantage being off-axis. It also reports that in full-vehicle simulation the RECTANGULAR section outperforms hexagonal and octagonal ones, which "undergo torsion and buckling", and concludes that "in practice, the benefits resulting from special cross section designs proved to be not cost efficient. Thus axial folding aluminium crash boxes show usually of a single or two-cell design with a rectangular cross section."

So PW is the production archetype. A rectangular box with a second cell and folding initiators is what the industry document describes as the production solution, arrived at here independently from the mechanism evidence. The same document's recommended lever, "the addition of material where required enables a significant performance improvement" and "reinforced corners increase the specific energy absorption capacity", is exactly what PG and PW do.

Foam could not be rebutted directly, because the archived foam source carries density and modulus but no plateau stress or densification strain, and building a crushable foam model would have meant inventing the constant that governs the answer. That exclusion is reported rather than worked around.

11.3 The finding that reframes the project: it is the attachment, not the tube

The plain square tube reference (same 683.8 g, same steel, no flanges) turned out to be far MORE angle-robust than the production-style baseline: retention 100 / 102 / 96 / 84 / 70 percent at 0 / 6.25 / 10 / 20 / 30 degrees against the baseline's 100 / 92 / 70 / 49 / 28, and +114 percent energy at 30 degrees.

That inverted the assumption the project was built on, so it was attributed properly with a two-by-two factorial rather than guessed at, because the plain tube changed both section shape and end attachment at once.

cell section flanges ratio 6.25 ratio 10 ratio 20 axial PCF
baseline rectangular yes 0.659 0.450 0.372 118.2
FA rectangular NO 0.881 0.779 0.495 203.9
FB square yes 0.846 0.509 0.401 119.7
R square NO 0.882 0.799 0.527 196.4

Removing the flange is worth +0.222 / +0.329 / +0.123 on fold ratio and up to +49.5 percent energy at 20 degrees, and it does that for either section shape. Making it square is worth +0.187 / +0.059 / +0.029 with the flange present and essentially nothing (+0.001 / +0.020 / +0.032) once the flange is gone.

The end attachment governs the oblique failure. Section shape matters only because the flange is soft.

P4_SOURCES.md C5 flagged the flange representation as an ASSUMPTION with stated limits. It is the most load-bearing assumption in the study. A real box bolts to a rail documented in SSP 520 as 2.0 mm inner member plus a 1.5 mm closing plate, much stiffer than the modelled 1.8 mm free-standing shell flange but not the perfectly clamped rim of FA. The 6.25 degree critical angle is therefore the pessimistic bound; a real installation sits between 6.25 and beyond 10 degrees. Section 5's headline number must be read that way.

11.4 Why the attachment cannot simply be stiffened

Two further studies closed this line.

A flange stiffness sweep found a clean monotonic trade with no window. Peak force runs 118.2, 132.5, 152.0, 200.7 kN for flanges of 1.8, 2.7, 3.6, 5.4 mm, while energy at 20 degrees runs 2.795, 3.410, 3.829 kJ. Every step buys angle performance and pays proportionally in head-on force. The mildest step already exceeds the plus 1 percent cap twelvefold, and beyond about three times thickness the flange stops acting as a fuse at all (5.4 mm gives essentially the no-flange result).

An attempt to decouple the two failed, as predicted in advance. The hypothesis was that a bead near the base, where 60 percent of the axial plastic work happens, could set the collapse load and cap peak force while the flange kept supplying rotational restraint. The prediction recorded before solving was that peak force would return near 118 kN if the hypothesis held. It did not: beads pulled peak force down only 8.0 and 3.5 percent against flange increases of 29 and 12 percent. A local trigger cannot relieve a constraint that is not where the trigger is.

So the flange trade is fundamental for this design family across all three routes tried.

12. Restated conclusion

What the project delivers.

  1. A quantified diagnosis: this box class stops folding at about 6.25 degrees in the modelled configuration, sharply (an order of magnitude steeper than the surrounding trend) and seed-independently (0.24 percent energy, 0.002 fold ratio between imperfection seeds).
  2. The mechanism: two plastic zones with a near-rigid middle, the loaded end being what changes, and the END ATTACHMENT being what governs it.
  3. A design rule that held across nine candidates: weaken precisely the part that was going to fail, and nothing else.
  4. A finding that contradicts the project's own founding assumption: deformation mode and energy absorption are largely independent levers.
  5. A working design. PW, at plus 5 percent mass and no worse peak force, removes the energy cliff (94 percent retention at 10 degrees against the baseline's 70), survives severe speed, and at the RCAR angle absorbs the impact in about a quarter less stroke than the baseline (Section 13).
  6. An honest industry comparison: PW reaches the low end of the published range, matching the foam-filled conical figure, against a harder baseline and under a constraint most of that work does not apply.

What it does not deliver. Parity with the multi-cell and hierarchical headline claims. A fix valid beyond about 20 degrees. Any claim about the exact Golf R part. And a validated absolute peak force at a given load angle, per the DECISIONS.md D3 licence.

The single most important caveat, which Section 11.3 established and which qualifies everything upstream of it: the 6.25 degree critical angle depends on a flange representation that was always labelled an assumption and turns out to dominate the result. The number is a lower bound, not a measurement.

13. The RCAR test, what it actually shows, and a claim I withdrew

BRIEF.md framed the payoff as energy kept out of the frame rail. The traced industry test makes that concrete. RCAR V2.5, read in full and archived, specifies 15 km/h at a 10 degree barrier angle with 40 percent overlap, and it is a damageability and repairability rating: what makes repair cheap is that the bolt-on crash box absorbs the impact so the rails never deform.

So the real requirement is not an SEA number. It is: absorb the impact inside your own stroke, at the test angle.

13.1 The claim this section used to make, and why it is wrong

An earlier version of this section reported that at 10 degrees the baseline FAILS this test and PW PASSES. That claim is withdrawn. It was an artefact of a post-processing window, and the error is worth reporting in full because it is the kind that survives review.

Section 3.5 fixes EA as the integral of force over a 60 mm crush window, inherited from the Phase A anchor, which was a 100 mm specimen crushed 60 mm. As a comparative metric that is correct and it is applied identically to both sides of every comparison in this report. But the Phase B and later runs drive a 100 mm stroke into a 180 mm box, and the pass/fail question above is a stroke question, not a window question. Every FAIL in the old table meant "did not reach 4.99 kJ within 60 mm", while the sentence underneath it claimed "within its 100 mm stroke". At the moment EA stopped accumulating, the baseline at 10 degrees was still reacting 58.8 kN and PW still 110.2 kN. Nothing had densified. The box was still absorbing; the metric had simply stopped counting. And 60 mm has no standing as a usable stroke here: it is 33 percent of this 180 mm box, where the anchor's 60 mm was 60 percent of its own length.

One caveat survives from the original section: a claim found in secondary sources that crash boxes must protect from 0 to 30 degrees could NOT be traced to primary regulation text (three retrieval attempts on UNECE R42 failed or returned the wrong regulation), so it is not used as a target anywhere.

14. The three open items, closed

Section 12 listed three untested routes. All three were then run, seventeen solves, and none displaces PW. What they add is a far stronger answer to the question the study kept circling: why not simply do what the published work does.

Real multi-cell was tested properly and it vindicates the literature on energy while exposing the cost that literature does not report. Two internal webs giving three chambers produce +52 percent axial energy and +83 percent at 10 degrees, the largest gain in the whole project, measured against this project's own production-representative baseline rather than a plain tube. They also produce +87 percent axial peak force, the largest violation in the whole project. Squeezed into the plus 5 percent mass budget it still lands at +31 percent. This is the third instance of a pattern already seen with candidate B and candidate TB, and the clearest: the published gains in this family are real and would be rejected by a peak force constraint those studies do not apply.

Gussets are three times more efficient than flange thickening and still do not clear the cap. Stiffening only the two walls that resist bending, next to the flange, converts peak force penalty into angle performance at a ratio of 5.8 against flange thickening's 1.8. The best variant misses the cap by 1.55 kN out of 119.4. Its one allowed re-tune, shortening the collar from 10 mm to 4 mm, cut collar material by 60 percent and reduced peak force by only 0.5 kN while halving the angle benefit. The penalty is caused by the presence of a stiff junction, not by its size, which is why shrinking it sheds the benefit and keeps the cost.

So the attachment lever is closed on four independent routes: removing the flange (+72 percent PCF), thickening it (monotonic trade, no window), stiff flange plus a base trigger (recovers 8 percent against 29 added), and gussets at two sizes (+2 percent over cap at best). The Section 11.3 finding stands and now carries a firm negative beside it: within this design family the attachment cannot be stiffened without paying more in head-on force than the constraint allows, because anything that resists the hinge at the joint also resists the fold that starts there.

Bead optimisation was deliberately not run. It is worth a few percent and only makes sense once the design it tunes is settled; running it before these questions were answered would have optimised a design that might not be the winner. It remains open as a choice, not an omission.

Final standing: PW is unchanged as the design outcome. Plus 5 percent mass, peak force inside the cap, energy cliff removed, gains at severe speed, and it cuts intrusion at the RCAR angle by about a quarter against the baseline.

15. Phase E: the designed experiment, and a response surface that failed

The one item Section 14 left deliberately open was optimisation. Every bead depth, position and count in this project was hand-picked, and a parameter study was the obvious next step. It is now done: 50 solves, 24 new design points at two angles plus one verification pair, with the design space, budget, constraints and stop conditions all declared in DECISIONS.md D4 before any solve ran. Full working in RESULTS_LOG.md C14.

Four factors, mass pinned at exactly plus 5.00 percent on every point by construction: web thickness (with web length compensating), bead depth, leading band position, band spacing. Latin hypercube, fixed seed. Objective: energy at 10 degrees, the RCAR angle. Constraint: axial peak force ratio within the declared 1 percent, which three of 24 points break.

Isight is installed on the build machine but unlicensed, so this is a scripted DOE built on the project's own deck generator, and it is described that way everywhere. The parameterisation could not have been done in Isight's templating component regardless, since it meshes an internal web and moves nodes to emboss beads.

15.1 PW's hand-picked parameters were already near optimal

PW ranks second out of twenty-five. The point that beats it, DOE01, does so by 0.83 percent, and is PW with a shallower bead (1.025 mm against 1.35) and a web within 2 percent of PW's. The threshold declared in D4.5 before the study ran was 3 percent, so the recorded finding is that hand-picking had already found the plateau, and it is not iterated past.

That is a smaller headline than an optimisation study hopes for and a more useful one than it usually gets: it converts "these parameters were chosen by judgement" into "these parameters were chosen by judgement and a 25-point study could not meaningfully improve them".

15.2 The response surface does not work, and the reason is the physics

diagnostic value
R2 on its own fitted data 0.715
Q2, leave-one-out cross-validation -8.35
held-out prediction of PW -9.70 percent error
surface optimum at the corner of all four ranges, predicting 8.5577 kJ

A Q2 below zero means the model predicts designs it has not seen worse than simply guessing the average. Reducing the model does not save it: linear only gives Q2 = 0.112, linear plus quadratic main effects gives -0.164.

The cause is a regime boundary inside the design space. The fold-versus-bend discriminator returns fold ratios from 0.520 to 0.830 across the 24 points: some designs fold, some bend, most are mixed. The objective is therefore not a smooth function of the four factors, and no polynomial in them can be. The respectable looking R2 was 15 terms memorising 24 points across a discontinuity.

15.3 The verification pair was spent refuting, not confirming

D4.4 allowed exactly one verification pair. Confirming an optimum I did not believe would have wasted it, so it was spent testing the surface, with the prediction written into the results log before the solve: the surface said 8.5577 kJ, I predicted 4.7 to 5.3 kJ from the three solved points nearest that corner.

Result: 5.0698 kJ. The surface over-predicted by 68.8 percent, and the design it identified as 60 percent better than PW is in fact 5.1 percent worse than PW. The prediction held.

15.4 The study found this project's own central result independently

DOE12 and DOE19 are classified BEND at fold ratio 0.520 and absorb 5.14 and 5.07 kJ, more than several points that fold visibly better, while the five worst absorbers sit mid-range at 0.532 to 0.569. A study built to tune bead geometry recovered Section 6.4's finding that deformation mode and energy absorption are largely separate levers, sharing no method with it beyond the solver. That is the strongest internal corroboration in the project.

15.5 And it closes the peak force question

Across all 24 points axial peak force spans 118.164 to 120.394 kN, a range of 1.9 percent, and the Pareto front is nearly flat: giving up 8.4 percent of energy buys back 0.22 percent of peak force. There is no useful trade. Peak force is effectively constant across the feasible design space and energy is the only axis that moves, which answers whether PW should have been tuned toward a quieter spike. It should not.
