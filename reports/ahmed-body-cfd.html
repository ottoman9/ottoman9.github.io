Ahmed Body External Aerodynamics: Validation, Drag Crisis, and a Spoiler Study in OpenFOAM

Author: Usman Bashir, BSc Metallurgical and Materials Engineering Date: 2026-08-16 Solver: OpenFOAM v2512, simpleFoam (steady), k-omega SST with wall functions

Internal working records cited in this report (for example DECISIONS, RESULTS_LOG and the sources ledger) are available on request.

1. Summary

I set out to answer three questions with one open-source CFD toolchain, in order, so that each answer had to be earned before the next one was allowed. First, can this toolchain reproduce a published experimental drag measurement on a standard automotive benchmark, with a mesh-independence study and a tolerance band written down before any solve ran? Second, if it can, does it put the benchmark's famous drag crisis (a sudden fall in drag as the rear slant is steepened past a critical angle) where the experiment puts it? Third, can I then use the validated setup to solve an actual design problem, adding a small roof spoiler to a high-drag configuration, and quantify what it buys?

The three headline results:

  1. Validation passed at both gate angles. On the finest mesh, Cd = 0.3179 at a 25 degree slant and Cd = 0.2904 at 35 degrees, inside the bands 0.287 to 0.347 and 0.267 to 0.327 respectively (RESULTS_LOG.md 1.B to 1.D). Both mesh ladders converged, and the model ranked the two angles the same way the experiment does. Getting there was not clean: the 35 degree result initially failed the band I had pre-declared, and Section 4 describes in full what I did about that and why the band was eventually re-anchored.
  2. The drag crisis was reproduced, and located to a 1.25 degree interval. Drag rises to a peak of Cd = 0.3339 at 28.75 degrees and falls 0.0290 across the next 1.25 degrees, so the crisis sits in 28.75 to 30 degrees against an experimental value of approximately 30 degrees (RESULTS\STAGE2_RESULTS.md Section 11.6). A second, independent signature, the collapse of the solution oscillation from 4.63 percent to 0.68 percent peak to peak, lands in exactly the same interval.
  3. A 2.0 mm roof trailing-edge strip cut drag by 8.42 percent at zero pitch, for a reason I did not predict. Deltas against a method-identical baseline were -0.0271 at 0 degrees of pitch, -0.0176 at 5 degrees, and +0.0306 at 15 degrees (RESULTS\STAGE3_RESULTS.md Section 1). Every spoiler case, including the harmful one, collapsed the baseline's 6.56 percent solution oscillation to about 0.65 percent. That is the pre-declared signature of a regime change, so the honest headline is not "a spoiler saved 8 percent" but "this spoiler trips a pre-crisis body into the post-crisis separated regime at every pitch tested, and pitch then sets the drag of the separated configuration".

The discipline that holds all three together is pre-registration: predictions, thresholds and pass bands were written into DECISIONS.md before the runs that would test them existed. Several of those predictions were refuted. They are reported as refuted in Section 9, because a scorecard with no failures in it is evidence that the scorecard was written afterwards.

2. The benchmark, and why I chose it

A car spends most of its highway fuel pushing air out of the way, and roughly 85 percent of a simplified car's drag comes from the low pressure pocket behind it rather than from the air hitting the front (P3_SOURCES.md A3). Shaping the back of a car is therefore worth more than shaping the front, and the shape of the back is where the interesting failure of intuition lives.

In 1984, Ahmed, Ramm and Faltin published a simplified car-like block that strips away mirrors, rotating wheels and engine bay flow so that one question can be isolated: what does the shape of the rear do to drag (P3_SOURCES.md A1). The body is a box with a rounded nose and a slanted rear window, the slant, whose angle is adjustable. It has been the standard benchmark for automotive CFD ever since.

The landmine in it is this. Common sense says a steeper, more fastback-like slant should shed air more smoothly than a steep van-like drop. The Ahmed body proves common sense wrong. Drag rises steadily as the slant angle increases, then near 30 degrees it suddenly falls, because the flow over the slant changes character completely (P3_SOURCES.md C5). Below the crisis the air mostly follows the slant, and two swirling ropes of air peel off its side edges, the C-pillar vortices, named for the roof pillar they trail from on a real car. Those vortices have low pressure cores that suck the slant surface pressure down and therefore add drag, and they strengthen as the angle steepens. Past the crisis the slant is too steep for the flow to follow, so it detaches right at the top, the C-pillar vortices collapse, and the wake becomes a single larger low-energy recirculation like the one behind a flat-backed van. That separated wake costs less drag than the strong-vortex case just before it.

I chose this benchmark for three reasons. It has a published experimental drag measurement to validate against, so the first stage can genuinely fail. It has a well-documented physical phenomenon whose location a solver either finds or does not, so the second stage is a real test of the tool rather than a demonstration of the tool. And it has a documented weak spot for the exact model class I could afford: ERCOFTAC's best-practice material for this case states that high-Reynolds wall-function treatments "generally do not predict separation at all" at 25 degrees (P3_SOURCES.md L2), so I entered the project with a named way to fail and said so in advance (DECISIONS.md D14).

The drag coefficient used throughout is the standard definition,

Cd = F_drag / (0.5 * rho * v^2 * A)

normalised on the raw width by height rectangle, A = 0.112 m^2 (P3_SOURCES.md B3), rather than the angle-dependent projected silhouette, so that Cd at different slant angles stays directly comparable across the sweep and against the literature.

One point of history that belongs here rather than in a footnote. The project's original brief named a CAD-embedded commercial tool, SolidWorks Flow Simulation, as the solver under study, and Stage 1 setup work had already begun in it. I reversed that decision on day one of execution (DECISIONS.md D12). The reason was audience: a scriptable, solver-level workflow with publishable dictionaries is what a research supervisor can actually inspect, and optimising for tool-name recognition was optimising for the wrong reader. The partial setup work was discarded as sunk cost rather than salvaged. All CAD, the domain sizing, the gate angles, the stilt decision and both validation bands survived the switch unchanged, because they are physics and geometry decisions rather than solver settings. BRIEF.md Sections 0, 2 and 7 still carry the original tool's name, because the promised rewrite of those sections never happened; the superseded wording is preserved there and marked, not deleted.

3. Methods

3.1 Geometry and CAD provenance

I built the body parametrically in SolidWorks through its COM API with a Python script (CAD\build_ahmed_half.py), so that any slant angle can be regenerated from one source of truth rather than clicked into existence. No geometry in this project was drawn by hand. The 25 and 35 degree half-models (CAD\Ahmed_half_25deg.SLDPRT, CAD\Ahmed_half_35deg.SLDPRT) were built and verified on 2026-08-11 (RESULTS_LOG.md 1.A), and the later sweep and refinement angles came from the same script.

Dimensions are corroborated against a curated ERCOFTAC case-description page covering overall length 1.044 m, width 0.389 m, height 0.288 m, slant length 222 mm, ground clearance 50 mm and stilt diameter 30 mm (P3_SOURCES.md B9). The original SAE dimensioned drawing itself was never opened directly: two attempts to reach the case page's own domain failed at the network level, and that gap is recorded rather than papered over. Residual uncertainty is limited to fine detail (exact stilt longitudinal positions, fillet radii), not the headline dimensions.

Three build choices matter downstream. The slant length is held at 222 mm and rotated about the roof trailing edge, so overall length varies with angle (1023.200 mm at 25 degrees, 1003.852 mm at 35 degrees). The nose is a single R100 fillet (P3_SOURCES.md B13). And the four support stilts are modelled (DECISIONS.md D11), because the experiment measured the body on its stilts; omitting them under-predicts total Cd by roughly 0.017 to 0.02 at 25 degrees (P3_SOURCES.md B10), which would have biased validation in the flattering direction. That choice returns in Section 4 as the single most consequential piece of bookkeeping in the project.

Measured from the model: full frontal area 0.112032 m^2, half-body 0.056016 m^2. This also resolved an apparent source discrepancy, since 0.112 and 0.115 m^2 differ by exactly the front stilt pair's silhouette.

3.2 Domain

Half-model with a symmetry plane at the centreline (DECISIONS.md D2), which halves the cell count for the same resolution. The cost is structural: a symmetry plane forces a perfectly mirror-symmetric solution, so no side-to-side wake asymmetry can appear. For mean Cd, the quantity I validate and sweep, that asymmetry largely cancels, and ERCOFTAC states a half body is sufficient for steady RANS on this case (P3_SOURCES.md L24). It would be the wrong choice for a study of wake bistability or yaw, which is why no such claim appears anywhere in my results.

Domain sized from best-practice guidance written for this benchmark specifically (DECISIONS.md D8): inlet 3 body lengths upstream, outlet beyond 5 body lengths downstream, half-width 1L, height 1.35L, giving a blockage ratio of 3.8 percent, inside the under-5 percent threshold. Exact extents used: inlet x = -3.132 m, outlet x = +6.264 m, top y = 1.409 m, side z = 1.044 m, symmetry at z = 0, and a no-slip ground at y = 0. Top and side boundaries are slip, representing an unbounded stream rather than tunnel walls. A single domain serves both gate angles, so the 25 versus 35 degree comparison is a controlled A/B rather than two separately tuned setups.

Speed convention: 40 m/s, Re = 2.784 x 10^6, matching the Lienhart and Becker LDA campaign whose wake field I compare against (P3_SOURCES.md D3), with kinematic viscosity set so the Reynolds number lands exactly.

3.3 Mesh ladder

Meshing follows OpenFOAM's own external aerodynamics route (DECISIONS.md D13): blockMesh fills the domain with cubes, snappyHexMesh cuts the body out, snaps the cut cells onto the surface, and grows prism layers along it. Two refinement boxes sit around the body and its near wake.

Rung blockMesh divisions Background cell Cells (25 deg, measured)
Coarse 144 x 22 x 16 65.3 mm 483,847
Medium 189 x 28 x 21 50.0 mm 1,020,869
Fine 243 x 36 x 27 38.8 mm 2,038,349

Every cell in the mesh shrinks by the same linear factor of about 1.29 per rung. The snappyHexMesh levels and the refinement box extents are held byte-identical across all three rungs, so the ladder tests "does the answer change as everything gets finer" rather than "does the answer change because the wake got relatively more refined". There is one deliberate exception: the near-wall prism stack (3 layers, 0.6 mm first layer, expansion 1.15, total 2.08 mm) is held fixed in absolute terms. If it shrank with the mesh, y+ would fall by 1.29 per rung and the fine rung would land its first cell in the buffer layer, where neither wall functions nor wall-resolved treatment is valid, and the ladder would silently become a comparison of three different wall models wearing the same name.

The ladder is sized to a compute ceiling, not to what the case deserves, and I state the gap plainly: published validations closest to this project ran 16.6 million cells and a 3.7 to 10.1 million three-mesh study (P3_SOURCES.md L8, L11), and the coarse rung at 483,847 cells sits about 3 percent below ERCOFTAC's stated 0.5 million floor for a wall-function half-body (P3_SOURCES.md L3).

3.4 Solver, turbulence model and extraction rules

Steady simpleFoam with k-omega SST and standard wall functions (nutkWallFunction on body and ground), first cell at roughly 0.3 mm off the wall (DECISIONS.md D14, D15). Steady rather than transient is a budget choice, not an accuracy choice, and D14 says so in those words before any result existed: a transient solve of this case costs one to two orders of magnitude more compute for the same mesh, against six Stage 1 runs plus a nine-angle sweep plus four spoiler cases. Both published OpenFOAM validations of this case also used steady simpleFoam (P3_SOURCES.md L8, L9).

Discretisation follows the shipped v2512 motorBike case with one deliberate upgrade: second-order bounded limitedLinear 1 on the turbulence equations in place of first-order upwind, because ERCOFTAC asks for second order and the turbulence transport is what carries the separation prediction that decides the whole question (P3_SOURCES.md L4, L15). residualControl is set at 1e-5 as a detector, not an expectation: steady RANS on separating bluff bodies is documented as entering a limit cycle rather than converging, with Ahmed-body residuals not dropping below about 1e-3 (P3_SOURCES.md L20). Every run goes to a fixed 2,000 iterations, which is what the canonical tutorial does.

The extraction rule is fixed in advance and applied identically to every rung, every angle and every spoiler case (DECISIONS.md D15): reported Cd is the arithmetic mean of the last 400 of 2,000 iterations, column 2 of postProcessing/forceCoeffs/0/coefficient.dat. If that window shows more than 2 percent peak-to-peak variation, the result is logged as oscillating and reported as a mean with its band printed next to it, never smoothed into a single clean number. Reference values are set once and never varied: magUInf 40, lRef 1.044 (the zero-slant length every literature value normalises on, not the physical length of the angled body), Aref 0.056016 m^2 so that the half-model Cd is directly full-body equivalent, and liftDir set explicitly because a CofR-only specification in v2512 would silently report a sideforce coefficient labelled as lift (P3_SOURCES.md L18). The body patch includes all four stilts, so stilt drag is inside every reported Cd by construction.

3.5 Compute

All production solves ran on a 16-core cloud VM (IBM Cloud, p3-solver-ibm, Frankfurt, 16 physical cores and 32 vCPU), with snappyHexMesh serial and simpleFoam on 16 MPI ranks using scotch decomposition. Typical medium-rung case: about 26 minutes end to end. The Stage 3 queue of four cases took 4 h 34 min; the Stage 2 sweep of five new cases took 2 h 10 min 50 s; the D16 investigation of eight cases took 2 h 12 min of solver time.

My productive solve compute came to about 15 US dollars, at approximately $1.06 per hour of VM uptime. The certified total I was actually billed was 34.23 US dollars, because I once left the cloud instance idle overnight after a session interruption stalled the copy-back; I record that here as an operational lesson, and it does not affect the solves themselves (P3_DEFENSE_BRIEF.md Section 2, consolidating the per-stage figures in RESULTS\D16_RESULTS.md Section 1, RESULTS\STAGE2_RESULTS.md Section 11.10 and RESULTS\STAGE3_RESULTS.md Section 10). That distinction is worth stating because it sets the honest boundary of everything below: this is a study designed to be defensible at a cost a student can pay, not one designed to be definitive.

4. Stage 1: validation, the band failure, and the re-anchoring

4.1 What Stage 1 had to prove

Stage 1 was written to buy trust, and to be capable of failing. Four criteria, all pre-declared in BRIEF.md Section 2 before any solve: the mesh ladder must show genuine convergence at both angles (shrinking, one-directional mesh-to-mesh change, with scatter to be named as scatter); the fine-mesh Cd at both angles must sit inside a pre-declared band; the model must rank the two angles the same way the experiment does; and the wake topology must qualitatively match a laser-Doppler anemometry reference at 25 degrees.

I chose 25 and 35 degrees because they bracket the approximately 30 degree crisis and because they are the two angles the Lienhart and Becker LDA campaign measured, so six runs buy both a force check and a wake check (DECISIONS.md D4).

35 degrees (RESULTS_LOG.md 1.C):

Both ladders converge: the change shrinks by a factor of 6.3 at 25 degrees and 8.5 at 35 degrees, monotonically and in one direction. This is convergence, not scatter, by the definition written down beforehand. Criterion 3 also passes, since both the experiment and the model put 25 degrees above 35 degrees, and ranking two angles correctly is a harder test to fake than matching one absolute number.

4.3 The band failure

Criterion 2 did not pass. Against the original bands, anchored on Ahmed's 1984 measurements at 60 m/s (0.26 to 0.32 at 25 degrees, 0.22 to 0.28 at 35 degrees), the 25 degree fine mesh passed at 0.3179 and the 35 degree fine mesh missed by 0.0104 (RESULTS\gate_check_20260812_ibmvm.md). The miss was not noise: the run was dead steady over its trailing window and the ladder had converged, so it was a bias.

The first thing I did was not touch the band.

4.4 The investigation (D16)

I wrote a ranked diagnosis from the literature and turned it into an eight-case matrix in which every case is the control with exactly one change, verified by directory diff before anything ran, with the expected direction and size of each change written down before the run existed, plus a four-part adoption criterion and a stop rule (DECISIONS.md D16, results RESULTS\D16_RESULTS.md). The control was the 35 degree medium rung at Cd = 0.2929.

Run Single change Observed Cd Delta Expectation met
R1 ERCOFTAC inlet turbulence values 0.2920 -0.0009 No, direction wrong
R2 Slip floor ahead of the body 0.2926 -0.0003 No
R3 Cross-section enlarged, blockage 3.81% to 1.71% 0.2883 -0.0046 Yes
R4 nutUSpaldingWallFunction instead of nutkWallFunction 0.2934 +0.0005 Yes
R5 Near-wall stack to 5 layers at 0.3 mm 0.2979 +0.0050 No, direction wrong
R6 Realizable k-epsilon instead of k-omega SST 0.3008 +0.0079 No, falsified outright
R7 First-order turbulence convection 0.2917 -0.0012 Yes
R8 R3's enlarged domain applied at 25 degrees 0.3244 +0.0027 vs the 25 deg control No, above the guard

Three of seven met their pre-declared expectation. Nothing was adopted, and the stop rule fired on its first branch. The best candidate, R3, closed about a third of the gap and then broke the other angle when tested there, which is exactly the failure mode R8 was budgeted to catch. Even naively stacking all seven deltas gives +0.0064, the wrong way.

The most informative single result is R6. Swapping to the k-epsilon family was the matrix's one chance to blame the turbulence model, and Cd rose by 0.0079 instead of falling while the solution went from 0.64 percent to 23.95 percent peak-to-peak. That falsifies the model-family explanation outright.

What the matrix bought is a sentence that was an assertion before it ran and is a measured statement afterwards: the residual is not attributable to free-stream turbulence, floor treatment, wall-function formulation, near-wall resolution, turbulence-model family, or convection scheme, because each was tested one at a time and none of them accounts for it.

4.5 The re-anchoring (D17) and its safeguards

That left two untested candidates, and the cheap one was the anchor itself. My bands were built on a 60 m/s experiment while I solve at 40 m/s, and the 40 m/s LDA campaign I match for Reynolds number never measured total drag at all (P3_SOURCES.md C11).

Meile et al. 2011 measured this exact geometry at this project's exact speed and Reynolds number, at both gate angles: Cd = 0.299 at 25 degrees and Cd = 0.279 at 35 degrees, read from printed data labels on a bar chart rather than interpolated off a curve, with the stilt tare loads subtracted (RESULTS\D16_RESULTS.md Section 6.1, P3_SOURCES.md L37). My Cd includes the stilts by construction, so the fair comparison adds a stilt allowance to their numbers first: Gant's thesis gives +0.017 to +0.019 Cd from paired with-stilt and without-stilt runs, consistent across five turbulence models (P3_SOURCES.md L30).

Angle Meile (no stilts) Stilt-adjusted anchor My fine-mesh Cd Delta
25 deg 0.299 0.316 to 0.318 0.3179 +0.000 to -0.002
35 deg 0.279 0.296 to 0.298 0.2904 -0.006 to -0.008

The 25 degree row is the check I did not tune. Applying Gant's allowance to Meile's 25 degree number lands my own 25 degree result almost exactly on the corrected anchor, with no free parameters.

The safeguards, and why this is not moving the goalposts. D16 wrote down three conditions for a legitimate band revision before Meile's 35 degree value was read, and all three were evaluated and met (RESULTS\D16_RESULTS.md Section 6.3): the revision must be justified by source quality alone, independent of which way the number came out; it must be applied to both angles in the same pass, since Meile measured both; and the record must state plainly that the gate result was already known when the band changed. The test I would apply to anyone else is whether the argument for the new anchor would have been just as strong the day before the result arrived. It would: a primary wind-tunnel campaign at my exact speed, Reynolds number and both gate angles beats a value read off a chart from a different campaign at 60 m/s. The I ratified the revision on 2026-08-13 (DECISIONS.md D17).

Bands in force, and the verdict:

Angle Anchor Band Fine-mesh Cd Verdict
25 deg 0.317 0.287 to 0.347 0.3179 PASS

No measured value changed. The same 0.3179 and 0.2904 reported before D16 opened are the numbers in this table. Only the band moved, and the superseded bands and anchors are preserved in DECISIONS.md D7, BRIEF.md Section 5 and RESULTS_LOG.md, not deleted.

A direction check worth stating: the literature reports that RANS generally under-predicts drag at 35 degrees (P3_SOURCES.md L23), and Meile's own Fluent RSM run under-predicts their own experiment there by about 1 percent. I appeared to over-predict, which is the wrong way round for a generic RANS bias and was part of why I suspected the anchor rather than the setup. Against the stilt-bookkept, same-Reynolds anchor, my 35 degree result reads about 2 to 3 percent low, which agrees with the community bias after all.

4.7 Gate verdict

All four criteria pass, and Stage 2 was authorised (RESULTS_LOG.md 1.F). Notably, the failure mode D14 pre-declared as the one I feared most, a massively separated wake at 25 degrees producing a backwards or flat ranking, did not occur.

5. Stage 2: the crisis hunt

5.1 Mesh choice for the sweep

A three-rung ladder at every sweep angle was unaffordable, so the sweep runs on one rung. I used the medium rung, roughly 1.02 million cells, being the coarsest rung that sits on the converged side of both ladders (DECISIONS.md D18). The brief's original wording said "coarse-but-calibrated", and I did not take that as licence to use the coarse rung: its jump to medium is 0.024 at 25 degrees and 0.021 at 35 degrees, large enough that a crisis hunt on that mesh would partly be a hunt for mesh error.

5.2 The sweep, and the refinement

Seven angles from 20 to 35 degrees in 2.5 degree steps, with the 25 and 35 degree medium results reused from Stage 1 (recomputed from their stored post-processing rather than copied, agreeing to 0.321702 against 0.3217 and 0.292852 against 0.2929). A later pass added 26.25 and 28.75 degrees, both built by cloning the 27.5 degree case and swapping only the STL, verified by diff -r returning zero differences on both the laptop and the VM and by md5 matching each placed geometry against its source.

The refinement moved the answer, and that is reported rather than folded in silently. The seven-angle sweep put the maximum at Cd = 0.3290 at 27.5 degrees and classified the crisis interval as 27.5 to 30 degrees. Solving the 1.25 degree midpoints showed that 28.75 degrees is higher still at Cd = 0.3339, so the rise continues through both new points and the fall starts one rung later than the sweep placed it. The 2.5 degree grid under-reported the peak by about 1.5 percent and mis-located it by 1.25 degrees. That is exactly the cost of the resolution limit that RESULTS\STAGE2_RESULTS.md Section 8 stated in advance, now measured rather than asserted. The superseded peak values are preserved in RESULTS_LOG.md under superseded values; they are not wrong measurements, they are correct measurements at angles that turned out not to contain the peak.

5.3 The crisis, at 28.75 to 30 degrees

The drop across 28.75 to 30 degrees is 0.0290, larger than the sweep's 0.0240 despite spanning half the angle, and it carries 71 percent of the total post-peak fall of 0.0410. Per degree it is 0.0232 against 0.0066 for the next largest step, so normalising for the now uneven grid does not rescue any competing interval.

Against the experimental crisis at approximately 30 degrees, the offset is 0 to 1.25 degrees early, with the interval's upper bound touching the experimental value. This is the Stage 2 success criterion and it is met: the solver puts the crisis at or just below the experimental angle, not several degrees early and not absent. I quote an interval and never a critical angle, because any single number would be an interpolation presented as a measurement.

Separability, using the yardsticks pre-declared in D18 rather than new ones: the drop is 3.3 times the half-sum of its two bounding oscillation bands, and 6.9 times the medium-to-fine mesh error measured at the crisis angle. Both ratios are better than the seven-angle sweep's 2.4 and 5.7, because the drop grew while the noise did not.

That mesh error is itself measured rather than assumed. A fine-rung case at 30 degrees (2,040,378 cells, Cd = 0.3008, 55 min 10 s) was run specifically because Stage 1 never showed that medium-to-fine convergence holds at the crisis angle, where the flow is changing regime and is most likely to be mesh sensitive. It holds: the medium-to-fine change at 30 degrees is 0.0042, against 0.0038 at 25 degrees and 0.0025 at 35 degrees.

5.4 The second, independent signature

Solution oscillation amplitude runs 5.75, 5.02, 5.36 and 4.63 percent across 25, 26.25, 27.5 and 28.75 degrees, a mild and noisy drift, then collapses to 0.68 percent at 30 degrees, a factor of 6.8, in the same 1.25 degree interval that carries the drag drop. Neither signature was tuned to the other, and they agreed on the coarser grid before they agreed on the finer one. The 30 degree steadiness also survives the doubling of cell count (0.78 percent on the fine rung against 0.68 on the medium), so it is a property of the flow regime rather than of the mesh.

What the finer grid removes is the tidier reading that amplitude declines steadily towards the crisis. It does not: it is a cliff confined to the last 1.25 degrees, not the end of a trend.

5.5 Three honest qualifications

The classification was not re-run on the refined grid. D18 gives the refinement pass one job, narrowing the interval, and re-evaluating pre-declared thresholds against a grid they were not written for, after seeing the result, is the post-hoc move D18 exists to prevent. One consequence must be said plainly: D18's condition A1 required the maximum to sit at an angle in {25, 27.5, 30}, and the refined maximum sits at 28.75 degrees, outside that set. A1 is not thereby failed, because it was written for and passed on the 2.5 degree sweep, but the sweep's peak angle was an artefact of the grid, and 28.75 degrees is the number to quote from now on.

One ambiguity test came close to firing and the threshold was not adjusted. D18's C2 test fires if two non-adjacent steps both exceed 0.010 and differ by less than 0.005. The two qualifying drops differ by 0.0076, so C2 misses by 0.0026. Had the second drop been 0.0190 instead of 0.0164, the sweep would have classified AMBIGUOUS. That margin is on the record rather than smoothed.

The curve is not monotonic above the peak. Cd recovers by 0.0043 from 30 to 32.5 degrees. That does not violate A3 as written, which measures against the peak value, and it is in any case 3.3 times smaller than the 32.5 degree case's own oscillation band, so it is not separable from solution unsteadiness. It is reported, not explained.

5.6 The depth of the crisis, an open observation

Location is one thing and depth is another, and I have only the first. My peak-to-plateau fall is 0.3339 minus 0.2929, equal to 0.0410. Ahmed's reproduced chart shows roughly 0.378 falling to 0.260, about 0.118, so I reproduce roughly a third of that depth while getting the location right to within 1.25 degrees (RESULTS\STAGE2_RESULTS.md Section 12). A constant stilt offset does not explain the gap, because adding the same allowance to both experimental values leaves the fall unchanged.

The complication is that my two experimental comparators disagree with each other. Meile's same-Reynolds pair spans only about 0.020 across the same two angles, against the chart's 0.118, a factor of nearly six. The experimental depth at this Reynolds number is therefore not settled in my source set, and I do not treat 0.0410 as validated against any single agreed experimental depth. Stated as an open observation, deliberately not resolved. The most likely physical reason for a shallow crisis in a steady RANS calculation is the known under-prediction of turbulence in the initial separated shear layer (P3_SOURCES.md L32), which is what a scale-resolving simulation would attack.

6. Stage 3: the spoiler study

6.1 Design

I took the high-drag 25 degree body, pre-crisis with strong C-pillar vortices, and fitted a 2.0 mm flat strip at the roof trailing edge at three pitch angles, 0, 5 and 15 degrees, following the geometry and pitch guidance of the source study (P3_SOURCES.md G2, G5, DECISIONS.md D10, D19). All thresholds, claim limits and the expected ordering were fixed in advance in D19.

Two design choices carry the result. The baseline was re-run, not reused. Every spoiler case carries a level 6 refinement box at the roof trailing edge and the old 25 degree case does not, so reusing it would have folded "we refined the roof" into a number labelled "the spoiler did this". The re-run came out at 0.3212 against the old 0.3217, a difference of 0.0005, so the box by itself did essentially nothing and the paired deltas are clean. The acceptance check for the plate is face count, not cell count. At the medium surface cell size the 2 mm plate is 0.64 of one cell, and a cut-cell mesher can lose a feature thinner than a cell and still return a plausible "no change", silently. Level 6 refinement gives 0.777 mm cells, 2.57 across the plate, and each spoiler case had to add 30,000 to 55,000 body patch faces over the baseline. Each added 38,000 to 39,000. A near-baseline face count would have voided the case.

The four cases share byte-identical system/, 0.orig and Allrun directories. The only file that differs anywhere across the four case trees is the STL.

6.3 The finding I did not predict

Every spoiler case, including the one that makes drag worse, collapsed the baseline's 6.56 percent oscillation band to 0.63 to 0.69 percent. D19 pre-declared, before any Stage 3 case ran, that a collapse below about 1.5 percent means the strip has pushed the body out of the oscillating pre-crisis state and the delta must be reported as a regime change, not as C-pillar vortex weakening. The signature fired on all three. A fine-tuning of vortices would not do that to a case that gets worse.

The pictures say the same thing in physical terms (RESULTS\stage3_surfaces_compare.png, RESULTS\stage3_cpillar_planes.png). The baseline's roof trailing edge is a strong suction stripe at Cp near -1.2, and that stripe is gone on every spoiler case; slant-face mean Cp rises from -0.375 to about -0.22 at 0 and 5 degrees and -0.255 at 15 degrees. The symmetry-plane wake then grows: reversed-flow area in the first 0.30 m behind the base goes from 18 percent to 50, 54 and 60 percent at 0, 5 and 15 degrees. On the transverse cuts, the baseline's concentrated C-pillar core at omega_x approximately -2450 /s drops to -406, -217 and +277 /s, a six-fold to eleven-fold collapse or a sign flip. So the strip both kills the roof-edge suction and opens a separated wake, and pitch decides which effect wins.

This also explains a number that would otherwise look like an error. My 8.4 percent is roughly three times what the source study reports, and the reason is not that my strip works three times better. The source ran a 35 degree body, already past the crisis, so it had little regime change left to buy. My 25 degree body had all of it. Comparing the two magnitudes directly is not meaningful, and the 0 degree Cd of 0.2942 sitting right on Stage 2's post-crisis values of 0.3050 and 0.2929 is the qualitative corroboration.

6.4 The fuel estimate, with its ranges and caveats

Power to overcome drag is P = 0.5 * rho * v^3 * Cd * A, evaluated at 120 km/h with rho = 1.225 kg/m^3, then divided by an assumed fuel-to-wheels efficiency of 25 percent, the midpoint of the EPA highway range of 22 to 30 percent (P3_SOURCES.md H4). For the best case, delta-Cd = -0.02706:

A provenance disclosure I volunteer rather than wait to be asked about. D19 originally blocked the litres figure until a sourced fuel energy-density row existed, and blocked the car-scale figure until a sourced frontal-area row existed. Both rows (P3_SOURCES.md H5, H6) were added on 2026-08-14, after the results were known, during a handover between work sessions. I ratified keeping both figures on 2026-08-16 knowing that ordering (DECISIONS.md D19, ratification note). That is weaker provenance than a row that existed before the result, and the printed caveats are the reason the figures are still defensible. This is the one place in the project where a pre-declared boundary was released after the fact, and it is disclosed here rather than left for a reader to discover.

What these numbers are not. They are the aerodynamic term only, at one speed, steady, straight, in still air. They are not a drive-cycle consumption and must not be compared against a published sticker figure. No percentage fuel saving is quoted anywhere, because a percentage needs a total fuel consumption I do not have; the -8.42 percent is a drag percentage and is labelled as one. The car-scale row assumes the same delta-Cd transfers to a real car, and nothing in this project demonstrates that: it is an area scale, not a vehicle validation.

7. What I would do next

In the order I would spend the compute, each item attacking a named weakness rather than making an existing answer prettier.

  1. A fine-grid study of the crisis depth. My crisis is in the right place and roughly a third as deep as one of my two disagreeing experimental comparators. Fine rungs at 28.75 and 32.5 degrees would test whether the peak location survives refinement (it is currently assumed from a measurement 1.25 degrees away), and would attack the 32.5 degree recovery, which is still unexplained.
  2. A DDES or hybrid RANS-LES comparison at 25 and 35 degrees. This is the largest single upgrade available. It attacks the one bias I could not eliminate, the steady-RANS turbulence deficit in the initial separated shear layer (P3_SOURCES.md L32); it would let me say something about the unsteady wake for the first time; and it is the most likely candidate to deepen the shallow crisis above.
  3. Drop the symmetry plane. A full model at the same resolution would let me claim the C-pillar pair as two solved vortices rather than one plus a mirror, and would open bistability and yaw, both currently forbidden by the half-model decision.
  4. A paired level 7 spoiler pass, the escalation D19 already specified and costed, so the plate's own boundary layer and trailing edge are resolved rather than just its two faces.
  5. A proper pitch sweep, five or six angles between 0 and 15 degrees, so the sign flip can be located instead of bracketed.

What I would not spend it on is more iterations. The mean is stable to 0.00009 while the solution orbit is 0.0185 wide, so doubling the iteration count would shrink an uncertainty component already worth about 0.0001 and move no digit anyone reads.

One literature item, not a compute item, is the highest-value thing left on the validation: a sourced stilt-drag number measured at 35 degrees specifically, which would close the extrapolation named in Section 8.

8. Limitations

Stated in full, without hedging, because every one of them constrains a claim made above.

Single-source validation anchor. Both current pass bands trace to one paper, Meile et al. 2011. The superseded anchor at least had two independent secondary reproductions of the underlying 1984 data. If Meile's measurement or their stilt-tare methodology carries an unflagged error, both gate angles inherit it together.

Stilt allowance transferred across angles. Gant's +0.017 to +0.019 Cd was derived from paired runs at 25 degrees only. Carrying it to 35 degrees is a physically reasoned extrapolation (the stilts sit in the constant-section mid-body, well clear of the slant, so their drag should not depend strongly on slant angle), graded MEDIUM confidence, not HIGH. It is not a second measurement. The one free check on it, applying the same allowance to Meile's 25 degree number and landing on my own 25 degree result with no tuned parameters, shows the method is not obviously broken but does not prove the transfer.

Steady RANS on a flow that limit-cycles. The solver never converges in the strict sense; it enters a stationary limit cycle, which is documented behaviour for this class of flow. I characterised it rather than assumed it away: the orbit is a fixed-amplitude cycle, not a drift, and the 400-iteration mean averages about 16 complete cycles. But a trailing mean of a steady solver's orbit is not a time average of the physical flow, and the two must never be presented as the same thing. No claim about wake unsteadiness, vortex switching, bistability or yaw appears anywhere in this report, and none is available from this method.

Crisis depth under-predicted. My peak-to-plateau fall of 0.0410 is roughly a third of the 0.118 read from Ahmed's reproduced chart, while my two experimental comparators disagree with each other by nearly a factor of six on what that depth should be. Location is a validated-adjacent claim. Depth is not a claim I have made or can make.

y+ range and a bookkeeping miss. nutkWallFunction is valid for 30 <= y+ <= 300, and the one measured distribution I have on record reads min 2.75, max 211, mean 30.3 on the body. The maximum is fine. The minimum is not: a first cell at y+ around 3 sits below the buffer layer, where the log-law the wall function assumes does not hold. Three qualifications, all of them mine to own. That measurement comes from a 100-iteration benchmark on the coarse rung, not from a converged production run, so it is indicative rather than certified for the rungs the gate rests on. D14 promised the measured y+ range would be logged for every rung and that logging was not done, which is a process miss in my own project, recorded in RESULTS_LOG.md 1.G rather than backfilled with numbers invented after the fact (the raw yPlus.dat files are on disk, so anyone can recompute it). And a low-y+ tail is expected wherever local skin friction is low, that is, in separated regions where the log-law was never going to hold anyway. Two things limit the damage: published submissions to this benchmark ran wall functions across y+ 11 to 550, and I tested wall treatment directly rather than arguing about it (D16 R4 moved Cd by 0.0005, R5 by 0.0050 in the wrong direction).

Strict-mode mesh check failures. Run with -allGeometry -allTopology, checkMesh reports four failures on every case: concave cells, cells with a small determinant, faces with interpolation weight below 0.05, and faces with volume ratio below 0.01. The standard non-orthogonality, skewness and aspect ratio checks all pass. These are layer-addition artefacts concentrated where the prism layers meet the surface refinement, which is the normal snappyHexMesh outcome. What makes them harmless to the comparisons, and only to the comparisons, is that every case in the project reports the identical set of four with mesh quality metrics agreeing to five decimal places across case sets built on different days, so the poor cells contribute the same error to both sides of every difference I claim. They are still poor cells, they sit near the wall where the physics is decided, and a project with more compute would fix them rather than document them.

Mesh resolution below the published norm. My fine rung is 2.04 million cells against published validations at 3.7 to 16.6 million, and my coarse rung sits about 3 percent below ERCOFTAC's stated floor for a wall-function half-body. A converged ladder proves the answer stopped moving under controlled refinement; it does not prove the answer is right. This bites hardest on absolute Cd, and least on the crisis location and the spoiler delta, where a systematic offset of consistent sign largely cancels in a difference.

Stage 3 absolute Cd is not comparable with Stages 1 or 2. All four Stage 3 meshes carry the level 6 refinement box; none of the earlier meshes do. Stage 3 claims a difference and only a difference. The one comparison drawn across that boundary, the 0 degree case sitting near the post-crisis sweep values, is explicitly qualitative.

Half-model consequences. One C-pillar vortex is computed and the other is an enforced mirror, drawn dashed and labelled as such in the figure. I do not claim a solved pair.

Fuel figures are model-scale only, and one boundary was released after the fact. The defensible figures are the model-scale ones at 0.112 m^2. The car-scale row is a labelled illustration built on a MEDIUM-confidence frontal area, and both the litres conversion and the car-scale row rest on source rows added after the results were known and ratified afterwards (Section 6.4).

Three angles is not a pitch sweep. I can say the sign of the spoiler's effect flips somewhere between 5 and 15 degrees of pitch, and nothing finer.
